What Is the Secret Ingredient in Pad Thai?

Tamarind is the sour ingredient behind the classic sauce. A traditional Pad Thai recipe blends sour, salty and sweet, according to the Thailand Foundation, and tamarind paste carries the sour side of that balance.

Tamarind, the Sour Fruit Paste Behind the Sauce

Tamarind grows as a brown pod on a tropical tree. Inside the pod, the University of Florida's IFAS Extension describes a sweet-sour, dark brown paste that surrounds two or three seeds.

That paste is the part cooks use. The same extension notes that it goes into Worcestershire sauce, soft drinks, chutneys and curries, so you may already have tasted tamarind.

In Pad Thai, the paste is cooked into the sauce. The Thailand Foundation describes the classic three-flavor sauce as stewed from tamarind paste, toddy palm sugar and fish sauce.

Why Tamarind Is Hard to Replace

Other sour ingredients can stand in for some of the sourness. The Thailand Foundation says the sourness of tamarind paste is the exception.

Tamarind paste is sweet and sour at the same time, and it is dark brown, so a sauce built on it can look and taste different from one built on sugar alone.

The Supporting Ingredients That Work With Tamarind

Two more ingredients complete the classic sauce, and a few extras add texture.

Fish Sauce: The Salt That Holds the Sauce Together

Saltiness in a traditional Pad Thai comes from fish sauce, which the Thailand Foundation calls a potent ingredient that needs to be used sparingly.

Palm Sugar: The Sweetness That Softens the Sour

Sweetness traditionally comes from coconut sugar, toddy palm or sugar cane. It tends to round off the tamarind so the sour edge feels softer.

The Quieter Classics: Dried Shrimp, Tofu and Pickled Radish

The Thailand Foundation's traditional recipe also adds dried shrimp, diced yellow tofu, pickled radish and pounded roasted peanuts. These add savory notes and texture to the noodles.

A slice of lime is also traditionally served alongside the dish, so each diner can brighten the plate at the table.

Why Pad Thai Tastes Different From Kitchen to Kitchen

Two plates with the same name can taste very different. The sauce balance is the biggest reason, and the noodle and the kitchen's own additions also matter.

The Sauce Leans Sweet: Sugar Doing Tamarind's Work

A sauce that relies on sugar, ketchup or a sweet chili sauce tends to taste sweet first and fade after a few bites. It can also take on a red or orange tint, where a tamarind-forward sauce usually looks brown.

The Noodle Changes the Texture

The noodle also changes the plate. The Thailand Foundation notes that some versions use sen chan, a thinner rice noodle. On our menu, the standard Pad Thai uses stir-fried rice noodles, and Pad Thai Woon Sen swaps in vermicelli, also called glass noodles, with the same egg, bean sprouts, green onions and crushed peanuts.

Each Kitchen Adds Its Own Touch

Regional cooks have their own versions. The Thailand Foundation describes an eastern-coast style seasoned with tamarind paste, cane or palm sugar and fish sauce.

On our menu, the Nakhon Luang Signature Pad Thai lists rice noodles stir fried with egg, lemongrass, bean sprouts and grilled chicken, topped with ground peanuts and green onion.

How the Pad Thai Sauce Balances Sour, Salty and Sweet

The classic sauce works because each ingredient answers another. This table shows what each one brings and what tends to happen when it is missing or overdone.

Ingredient What It Adds When It Is Missing or Overdone
Tamarind paste Fruity sourness and a brown color The plate tends to taste sweet and flat
Fish sauce Saltiness and depth Too much can bury the tamarind
Palm sugar Sweetness that rounds off the sour Too much can turn the dish candy-sweet
Lime on the side A fresh, bright finish at the table Each diner can add more or skip it

Some diners like a sharper, more sour plate and others prefer it sweeter, which is why the lime is served separately.
